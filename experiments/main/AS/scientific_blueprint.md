# Scientific Blueprint: Aortic Stenosis

## 1. Condition Overview

**Definition and Pathophysiology**
Aortic Stenosis (AS) is the narrowing of the aortic valve opening, which restricts blood flow from the left ventricle (LV) to the aorta during ventricular systole. This obstruction causes a pressure gradient, leading to turbulent blood flow (the source of the acoustic murmur) and compensatory left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH). 

**Clinical Significance**
AS is a progressive, life-threatening condition. If left untreated, severe symptomatic AS has a high mortality rate. Accurate auscultation is critical for early detection and severity grading.

**Subtypes and Demographics**
*   **Calcific AS:** Age-related progressive calcification; most common in patients >65 years (prevalence ~2–7%).
*   **Congenital Bicuspid AS:** A structural abnormality where the valve has two leaflets instead of three; typically presents in younger patients (40–60 years).
*   **Rheumatic AS:** Consequence of rheumatic fever; usually accompanied by mitral valve disease.

## 2. Signal Manifestation

In the audio domain (phonocardiogram/PCG), Aortic Stenosis is characterized by a distinct systolic murmur and specific alterations to the fundamental heart sounds (S1 and S2).

### Acoustic Event Structure
*   **S1 (First Heart Sound):** Marks the beginning of systole. Usually normal in intensity, though it may be masked by a loud murmur.
*   **Ejection Click (EC):** A high-pitched, early systolic transient sound occurring 40–60 ms after S1. Present primarily in bicuspid aortic valves; absent in severely calcified, immobile valves.
*   **Systolic Murmur:** The hallmark of AS. It is an ejection systolic murmur occurring between S1 and S2.
*   **S2 (Second Heart Sound):** Marks the end of systole. Composed of A2 (aortic closure) and P2 (pulmonic closure). In AS, A2 becomes progressively softer and delayed.
*   **S4 (Fourth Heart Sound):** A low-frequency presystolic sound occurring ~70–100 ms before S1, caused by atrial contraction into a stiff, hypertrophied left ventricle.

### Temporal Organization and Timing Patterns
*(Assuming a resting heart rate of 60 BPM; R-R interval = 1000 ms; Systole ≈ 350 ms)*
*   **S1 Duration:** 50–100 ms.
*   **Murmur Onset:** Begins shortly after S1 (or immediately after the EC), leaving a brief silent gap (20–40 ms) between S1 and the murmur.
*   **Murmur Duration:** 200–300 ms. Crucially, the murmur **ends before S2**.
*   **S2 Timing:** Occurs at the end of the murmur. In severe AS, A2 is delayed, causing a single S2 or paradoxical splitting (P2 occurs before A2).

### Spectral Characteristics and Dominant Frequency Bands
*   **S1 / S2 / S4:** Low-frequency transients. Dominant energy in the **20–100 Hz** band.
*   **Ejection Click:** Mid-to-high frequency transient. Dominant energy in the **150–400 Hz** band.
*   **AS Murmur:** A harsh, raspy, mid-to-high frequency broadband noise. 
    *   Dominant frequency band: **150–500 Hz**.
    *   Peak energy typically centers around **200–300 Hz** (est.).
    *   Harmonics/upper-frequency roll-off can extend up to **800 Hz** in severe, high-velocity jets.

### Envelope and Amplitude-Modulation Behavior
*   **Murmur Envelope:** The AS murmur has a classic **crescendo-decrescendo (diamond-shaped)** amplitude envelope. 
*   The amplitude rises smoothly to a peak (crescendo) and falls smoothly (decrescendo) before S2.
*   The mathematical shape of this envelope closely resembles a skewed Gaussian or a Hanning window.

### Recording and Acquisition Variability
*   **Spatial Location:** The murmur is loudest at the Right Upper Sternal Border (RUSB) (2nd intercostal space, right of the sternum).
*   **Radiation:** The high-frequency components radiate strongly to the carotid arteries (neck). A microphone placed on the neck will capture the AS murmur clearly, which is a key diagnostic feature.
*   **Transducer:** Best captured using the diaphragm of a stethoscope (which acts as a mechanical high-pass filter, attenuating frequencies <100 Hz to highlight the murmur).

## 3. Severity and Variability

### Changes with Condition Severity
The acoustic properties of AS change predictably as the valve area decreases and the pressure gradient increases:

1.  **Envelope Peak Timing (The most critical severity marker):**
    *   *Mild AS:* The murmur peaks early in systole (approx. 30–40% of the way through systole).
    *   *Moderate AS:* The murmur peaks in mid-systole (approx. 50%).
    *   *Severe AS:* The murmur peaks late in systole (approx. 60–75%), reflecting prolonged ventricular ejection time.
2.  **S2 (A2) Amplitude:**
    *   *Mild:* Normal A2 amplitude.
    *   *Severe:* A2 becomes heavily attenuated or completely absent due to rigid, immobile valve leaflets.
3.  **S2 Splitting:**
    *   *Mild/Moderate:* Normal physiological splitting (A2 precedes P2 during inspiration).
    *   *Severe:* Paradoxical splitting (P2 precedes A2; splitting widens on expiration).
4.  **S4 Presence:** Becomes highly prominent in severe AS due to advanced LVH.

### Sources of Natural Variability
*   **Beat-to-Beat (Arrhythmias):** In patients with Atrial Fibrillation, the intensity of the AS murmur varies beat-to-beat. A longer preceding R-R interval results in a louder murmur on the subsequent beat due to increased ventricular filling.
*   **Respiration:** Unlike right-sided murmurs, AS (a left-sided murmur) does *not* significantly increase in intensity during inspiration. It may slightly increase during expiration.
*   **Body Habitus:** Obesity or COPD (hyperinflated lungs) acts as an acoustic low-pass filter and attenuator, reducing the overall amplitude and high-frequency content of the murmur by up to 10–15 dB (est.).

## 4. Key Discriminators

### Top 4 Discriminative Features
1.  **Envelope Shape:** Diamond-shaped (crescendo-decrescendo) vs. flat/pansystolic.
2.  **Murmur Termination:** Ends *before* S2, leaving S2 audible (unless A2 is absent). Pansystolic murmurs obliterate S2.
3.  **Peak Timing:** The shift of the amplitude peak from early to late systole is unique to ejection murmurs.
4.  **Carotid Radiation:** The presence of the murmur in audio captured at the neck strongly isolates AS from other systolic murmurs.

### Differential Diagnosis Table

| Feature | Aortic Stenosis (AS) | Mitral Regurgitation (MR) | Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM) | Pulmonary Stenosis (PS) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Envelope Shape** | Crescendo-Decrescendo | Pansystolic (Flat/Plateau) | Crescendo-Decrescendo | Crescendo-Decrescendo |
| **Frequency Band** | 150–500 Hz (Harsh) | 100–400 Hz (Blowing) | 100–300 Hz | 150–400 Hz |
| **Murmur Timing** | Ends *before* S2 | Extends *through/past* S2 | Ends *before* S2 | Ends *before* or *at* S2 |
| **S2 Characteristics** | A2 soft/absent, paradoxical split | Widely split | Normal | Widely split, soft P2 |
| **Radiation** | To Carotids (Neck) | To Axilla (Armpit) | Minimal radiation | Minimal radiation |
| **Valsalva Maneuver** | Murmur intensity *decreases* | Murmur intensity *decreases* | Murmur intensity **increases** | Murmur intensity *decreases* |

## 5. Synthesis Considerations

### Recommended Computational Approach
To simulate AS, a **subtractive synthesis** approach combined with **precise amplitude modulation (AM)** is highly recommended. 

1.  **Base Heart Sounds (S1, S2, S4):** Generate using short bursts of low-frequency noise or single-cycle sine waves (e.g., 50 Hz) passed through a steep low-pass filter (cutoff ~100 Hz) and shaped with a fast-attack, exponential-decay envelope.
2.  **Murmur Generation:** 
    *   Source: Generate broadband Pink Noise (to simulate turbulent fluid dynamics).
    *   Spectral Shaping: Apply a bandpass filter.
    *   Temporal Shaping: Apply a skewed Gaussian AM envelope.

### Concrete Parameter Ranges for DSP Engineers

*   **Heart Rate (HR):** Default to 60–70 BPM.
*   **S1/S2 Base:** Center frequency $F_c = 40$ Hz, Bandwidth $= 40$ Hz. Duration $= 60$ ms.
*   **Murmur Carrier (Pink Noise):**
    *   Bandpass Filter $F_c$: 250 Hz (Range: 150–400 Hz).
    *   Filter Q-factor: ~1.5 to 2.0 (broad enough to sound harsh, not tonal).
*   **Murmur AM Envelope (The "Diamond"):**
    *   Use a generalized Gaussian or Beta distribution curve.
    *   *Onset:* 30 ms after S1 ends.
    *   *Offset:* 20 ms before S2 begins.
    *   *Peak Time (Mild AS):* 35% of the murmur duration.
    *   *Peak Time (Severe AS):* 70% of the murmur duration.
*   **Amplitude Ratios (Linear scale, relative to S1 = 1.0):**
    *   Mild AS Murmur Peak: 0.5
    *   Severe AS Murmur Peak: 1.5 to 2.0
    *   Mild AS A2: 1.0
    *   Severe AS A2: 0.1 to 0.0 (effectively muted).
*   **S4 (if simulating severe AS):**
    *   $F_c = 30$ Hz. Duration $= 50$ ms. Occurs 80 ms before S1. Amplitude $= 0.3$.

### Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them
*   **Pitfall 1: Making the murmur pansystolic.** If the murmur envelope overlaps with or extends past S2, the simulator will sound like Mitral Regurgitation. *Solution:* Ensure a strict mathematical zero-crossing/clamping of the murmur envelope at least 10–20 ms before the onset of S2.
*   **Pitfall 2: Tonal or "whistling" murmurs.** Using white noise with a high-Q bandpass filter will create a ringing, tonal sound (like a flute). AS is a harsh, turbulent sound. *Solution:* Use Pink noise (which naturally rolls off high frequencies) and a low-Q bandpass filter.
*   **Pitfall 3: Static S2 in severe AS.** A common mistake is leaving S2 loud and crisp while making the murmur severe. *Solution:* Link the murmur's peak-delay parameter inversely to the A2 amplitude parameter. As the peak shifts right (later), A2 amplitude must drop.
